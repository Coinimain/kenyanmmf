**The best shares to buy in Kenya are not always the cheapest shares or the ones rising fastest.** A good investment starts with a company you understand, a price you can justify and a plan for what could go wrong.

The Nairobi Securities Exchange (NSE) lists businesses in banking, telecommunications, energy, manufacturing and other sectors. You can become a shareholder in these companies without buying the entire business. But choosing which shares to buy requires more than checking today's price.

**Quick answer:** Before investing, check **earnings, the price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio, the price-to-book (P/B) ratio, return on equity, debt, cash flow, dividends, analyst valuations, trading liquidity, business risks, your investment horizon and total costs**. No single figure can reliably identify a winning share.

For current prices and recent market performance, use our [NSE Market Dashboard](/nse-market-dashboard/). This guide focuses on the principles that remain useful when prices change.

## What are shares, and how do you make money from them?

A share is a small ownership stake in a company. Buying 100 shares of a listed company makes you an investor in that business. It does not give you control of its daily operations, but it can entitle you to dividends and other shareholder rights.

Investors generally seek two kinds of return:

- **Capital gains:** You buy shares at one price and later sell at a higher price.
- **Dividends:** A company distributes part of its profits to eligible shareholders. Dividends are not guaranteed and can change or stop.

Suppose you buy **100 shares at KSh50 each**, investing KSh5,000 before charges. You later sell them at **KSh60 each**. Your gross capital gain is **KSh1,000**, or **20%** of the purchase value. Brokerage charges, levies and any applicable taxes reduce the amount you keep. If the share falls to KSh40 and you sell, you instead make a capital loss.

Your **total return** considers both price changes and dividends, after costs. A company with a falling share price can still pay dividends, but the dividend may not offset the price loss.

## Buy the business, not just the share price

Here is a practical point to remember: evaluate a share as though you were considering buying the whole business. Two important metrics to check are the P/E and P/B ratios.

However, those two figures are not the whole analysis. A business with weak cash flow, heavy debt or declining earnings can look inexpensive on paper and still be a poor investment.

Ask yourself: **Would I want to own this company if I could not sell its shares for several years?** If you cannot explain how it makes money, who its customers are and what could damage its profits, do more research before buying.

## 1. What is the P/E ratio? Meaning, formula and example

The **price-to-earnings ratio**, often searched as *PE ratio*, compares a share's price with the company's annual earnings per share (EPS).

**P/E ratio = Share price ÷ Earnings per share**

For example, if a share costs **KSh40** and the company earns **KSh8 per share per year**, its P/E ratio is:

**KSh40 ÷ KSh8 = 5 times**

That means the market is pricing the share at five times its annual earnings. Investors sometimes describe this as paying for five years of earnings. It is a useful analogy, **not a promise that your money will be repaid in five years**. Companies may retain profits, earnings may rise or fall, and the share price may change.

### What is a good P/E ratio for stocks in Kenya?

There is no magic number. A lower P/E may indicate that a share is cheaper relative to its earnings, but it can also reflect trouble ahead. A higher P/E may reflect expectations of stronger growth, or investors may simply be overpaying.

Check the following before deciding that a low P/E is attractive:

- **Compare peers:** A bank's P/E is more useful when compared with other banks than with a fast-growing technology business.
- **Check the earnings period:** A *trailing P/E* uses past earnings. A *forward P/E* uses forecasts that may be wrong.
- **Remove unusual gains:** A profit from selling property or reversing a large provision may not happen again.
- **Check profit direction:** If earnings are falling, last year's low P/E may be misleading.

A company reporting losses may have a negative EPS. In that case, a conventional positive P/E is not meaningful. Do not treat a negative or missing P/E as a bargain.

## 2. What is price-to-book value? P/B meaning and formula

The **price-to-book ratio** compares what investors pay for a company with the net assets recorded on its balance sheet.

**P/B ratio = Share price ÷ Book value per share**

**Book value = Total assets − Total liabilities**

Assume a company has a book value of **KSh50 per share** and its share price is **KSh40**:

**KSh40 ÷ KSh50 = 0.8 times**

The share trades at **20% below its stated accounting book value**.

| P/B ratio | What it means | What to check next |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | Price is 50% below book value | Are the assets reliable and profitable? |
| 0.8 | Price is 20% below book value | Why is the market applying a discount? |
| 1.0 | Price equals book value | What return does the business earn on those assets? |
| 1.5 | Price is 50% above book value | Does strong profitability justify the premium? |
| 2.0 | Price is twice book value | Are growth and future returns strong enough? |

**Important:** Book value is an *accounting measure*, not a guaranteed sale price or a company's true market value. Assets may be difficult to sell, overvalued on the balance sheet or unable to generate acceptable returns. Conversely, a successful business may deserve a premium because it earns strong profits from its assets.

P/B is particularly useful when studying **banks**, but it should still be used alongside profitability, asset quality and capital strength.

## 3. Use P/E and P/B together, then check return on equity

This is where many investors stop too early. A share that looks cheap on both ratios may be undervalued, or it may be a struggling business.

Consider two **fictional Kenyan banks**:

| Measure | Bank A | Bank B |
|---|---:|---:|
| P/E ratio | 4.0 | 8.0 |
| P/B ratio | 0.5 | 1.3 |
| Return on equity (ROE) | 5% | 19% |
| Profit trend | Falling | Growing |
| Main concern | Weak asset returns | Higher purchase price |

Bank A looks cheaper. But its low ROE and declining profits suggest there may be a reason for the discount. Bank B costs more yet produces a much stronger return on shareholder equity.

**ROE = Annual profit attributable to ordinary shareholders ÷ Average ordinary shareholders' equity × 100**

For a bank, also investigate non-performing loans, loan-loss provisions, capital adequacy and exposure to risky borrowers. High ROE driven mainly by excessive leverage is not automatically healthy.

The lesson is simple: **a low valuation is most attractive when the underlying business is sound**.

## 4. Check whether earnings are growing

A single profitable year is not enough. Read several years of annual results and the latest interim statements. Look for growth in revenue, operating profit, net profit and EPS.

Ask:

- Are profits rising because the business is selling more, or because of a one-off accounting gain?
- Are operating costs growing faster than revenue?
- Is the number of shares increasing and diluting earnings per share?
- Is profit growth supported by real cash flow?

For example, **Equity Group (EQTY), KCB Group (KCB), Co-operative Bank (COOP) and Diamond Trust Bank (DTK)** can be studied with similar banking measures. Their actual figures change with each reporting period, so compare the latest published results rather than copying an old P/E screenshot.

## 5. Examine cash flow, debt and the balance sheet

Profits are an accounting measure. Cash is needed to fund operations, repay creditors and pay dividends.

For non-financial companies, compare **operating cash flow** with reported profit. Check how much cash remains after maintaining and expanding the business. Review debt maturities, interest costs, receivables and large capital-expenditure commitments.

Banks require a different approach: deposits and loans are central to their business. Focus on loan quality, funding stability, regulatory capital, liquidity and credit-loss provisions instead of applying a manufacturer's free-cash-flow checklist without adjustment.

A low P/E can be a warning sign if earnings are strong on paper but the company cannot turn sales into cash.

## 6. Assess dividends, not just dividend yield

A dividend yield tells you how much annual dividend income you receive relative to the share price, **assuming the dividend is maintained**.

**Dividend yield = Annual dividend per share ÷ Share price × 100**

If a share trades at **KSh50** and pays **KSh2 per share** over a year, the indicated yield is **4% before tax and charges**.

But a high yield can appear when the share price collapses. That does not make the dividend safe. Check:

- Whether dividends have been regular or interrupted.
- The **payout ratio**: dividends per share divided by earnings per share, where meaningful.
- Whether profits and cash flows can support the payment.
- The **ex-dividend date**, record date and actual payment date.

Dividend investors should also consider the opportunity cost of holding a risky share instead of a [money market fund](/) or [Treasury bond](/blog/kenya-government-bonds-treasury-bonds-guide/). For the practical process, see our [NSE dividend-investing guide](/blog/ziidi-trader-dividend-investing-nse/).

## 7. Compare the share price with an analyst's fair-value estimate

An **analyst value gap** shows the difference between a share's current market price and an analyst's estimated fair value.

**Analyst value gap (%) = (Estimated fair value − Current share price) ÷ Current share price × 100**

Suppose an analyst estimates that a share is worth **KSh60**, while it trades at **KSh40**:

**(60 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = +50%**

A **positive gap** suggests potential price upside *if the valuation is correct*. A **negative gap** means the current price is above that analyst's estimate, suggesting possible downside.

| Value gap | Interpretation | Does it guarantee a return? |
|---|---|---|
| +30% | Analyst sees estimated upside | No |
| +5% | Small estimated upside | No |
| 0% | Price matches the estimate | No |
| −10% | Price exceeds the estimate | No |

Analyst targets are opinions built on assumptions about growth, interest rates, profitability and risk. Different analysts may reach different values for the same company. Check **when the target was published** and **why the analyst believes the gap will close**.

## 8. Check share liquidity and trading volume

Not every NSE share trades actively. You might identify an apparently cheap company but struggle to buy or sell a meaningful quantity at your preferred price.

Before placing an order, review the **average daily trading volume**, value traded and **bid-ask spread** (the difference between the highest buying offer and the lowest selling offer). A wide spread increases the cost of entering and exiting a position.

For a medium-term investor who may need to sell within **6–18 months**, liquidity matters nearly as much as the valuation. A calculated gain is not money in your pocket until you can complete the sale.

Use the [NSE Market Dashboard](/nse-market-dashboard/) to start exploring share prices and market activity. Confirm the current order-book prices and available quantities with your trading platform before submitting an order.

## 9. Understand the industry and the risks around it

Different companies need different research questions:

| Type of NSE company | Examples to research | Questions that matter |
|---|---|---|
| Banks | EQTY, KCB, COOP, DTK | Are loans performing, profits sustainable and capital buffers adequate? |
| Telecommunications | Safaricom (SCOM) | Are service revenue, M-PESA activity, margins and expansion economics improving? |
| Electricity | Kenya Power (KPLC), KenGen (KEGN) | How do tariffs, regulation, fuel costs, receivables, debt and capital spending affect profits? |
| Manufacturing and consumer businesses | Companies in the relevant NSE sector | Can they defend margins against imported inputs, power costs, competition and currency changes? |

Company examples are for **research**, not current buy recommendations. Check their latest disclosures and the relevant security's listing status.

## 10. Look for a credible reason the share could rise

Buying an undervalued share may require patience. The market needs a reason to reassess its price. Possible catalysts include stronger earnings, lower credit losses, reduced debt, improved cash flow, better dividends or clearer business prospects.

Do not assume a share will rise simply because it once traded at a higher price. And do not buy solely because a well-known investor, politician or social media account owns it.

A company can remain undervalued for years. Your investment case should explain **what might change**, **when you expect it** and **what evidence would prove you wrong**.

## 11. Match the share to your time horizon and risk tolerance

**Short-term traders** focus heavily on price movements, spreads and execution. **Medium-term investors**, often looking 6–18 months ahead, typically need a combination of reasonable valuation, earnings prospects, liquidity and possible catalysts. **Long-term investors** may place more weight on durable profitability and reinvestment.

Shares can suffer sharp losses even when the company is fundamentally strong. Money required for rent, fees, emergencies or another near-term obligation should not depend on selling shares at a favourable price.

Avoid concentrating all your money in one company or several businesses exposed to the same risk. Owning five banks may look diversified by stock name, but those banks can still react similarly to economic and interest-rate pressures.

For a broader starting point, read our [beginner's guide to investing in Kenya](/blog/complete-beginner-guide-to-investing-in-kenya/).

## 12. Calculate transaction costs, tax and your exit price

A share must rise enough to cover buying and selling costs before you earn a net profit. Ask your broker or approved trading platform for a **complete cost breakdown**, including brokerage, exchange and depository charges and any other applicable levies.

Tax rules also matter. In Kenya, dividend withholding tax can depend on the recipient and the nature of the dividend. Tax treatment of gains differs by asset and transaction. Check the latest [KRA withholding tax guidance](https://www.kra.go.ke/individual/filing-paying/types-of-taxes/individual-withholding-tax) and [capital gains tax guidance](https://www.kra.go.ke/individual/filing-paying/types-of-taxes/capital-gains-tax) rather than using an old rate from social media.

Check the current settlement and withdrawal process, too. A completed share sale does not always mean the cash is immediately available to withdraw. Our [NSE settlement guide](/blog/nse-settlement-t-plus-3-explained-ziidi-trader/) provides more detail; your broker should confirm current timings.

## A practical NSE share-selection checklist

Before buying, complete this short research sheet for **each** company you are considering:

| Check | What to record | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Business | What it sells and why customers buy | You cannot explain how it earns money |
| 2. P/E | Trailing and, if credible, forward P/E | Artificially low ratio from one-off profits |
| 3. P/B | Price relative to stated net assets | Weak or questionable assets |
| 4. ROE | Return earned on shareholder equity | Consistently poor returns or excess leverage |
| 5. Earnings | Several years of EPS and profit trends | Falling or erratic profits |
| 6. Cash and debt | Cash generation, borrowings, obligations | Unsustainable debt or weak cash collection |
| 7. Dividends | Yield, coverage, payment history | High yield unsupported by earnings |
| 8. Fair value | Analyst estimate, assumptions and date | Old target or unexplained optimism |
| 9. Liquidity | Trading volume and bid-ask spread | Very little trading or a wide spread |
| 10. Business risk | Sector, regulation, governance | Material unresolved threats |
| 11. Investment case | Reason to buy, horizon, exit conditions | Buying only because the price is rising |
| 12. Net return | Charges, taxes and cash needed | Tiny expected gain after costs |

You do not need every metric to be perfect. You do need to understand the trade-offs. A low P/E and low P/B become much more persuasive when earnings are sustainable, the balance sheet is sound and there is a credible path to better results.

## Worked example: Is this NSE share worth buying?

Imagine a **fictional company called Example Holdings** with the following figures:

| Measure | Hypothetical value |
|---|---:|
| Share price | KSh40 |
| Earnings per share | KSh8 |
| Book value per share | KSh50 |
| Annual dividend per share | KSh2 |
| Analyst fair value | KSh60 |
| P/E | 5.0 times |
| P/B | 0.8 times |
| Dividend yield | 5.0% |
| Analyst value gap | +50% |

At first glance, these figures look attractive: the company trades below book value, has a low earnings multiple, offers a dividend and has a positive analyst value gap.

**But the buy decision is still incomplete.** What if half of the earnings came from a one-time property sale? What if the company's borrowings are due next year? What if the share rarely trades? The apparent discount might disappear when you account for those risks.

A better decision comes from confirming that profits can be repeated, assets are dependable, debt is manageable and the share is sufficiently liquid for your needs.

## How to research and buy NSE shares in Kenya

You do not have to be a professional analyst to begin. Use a consistent process:

1. **Build a watchlist.** Start with a few companies you understand, perhaps one bank, one telecommunications company and one energy company.
2. **Check prices and trading activity.** Open our [NSE Market Dashboard](/nse-market-dashboard/) and then verify order-book details on your trading platform.
3. **Read financial reports.** Look for the latest annual report, interim earnings, dividend notices and public announcements from the company and the NSE.
4. **Calculate the key ratios.** Record P/E, P/B, ROE, dividend yield and the assumptions behind any estimated fair value.
5. **Write a one-paragraph investment case.** State why the share might be undervalued, what could go wrong and when you would review the decision.
6. **Use a licensed intermediary.** The [Capital Markets Authority's investor guide](https://www.cma.or.ke/investor-information/) recommends using CMA-licensed institutions and opening the relevant CDS account. Our [guide to opening a CDS account](/blog/how-to-open-cds-account-kenya/) explains the process.
7. **Review the order before buying.** Confirm the company name, share class, limit price, quantity, fees and total amount. Monitor the company after investing.

You can also review our [Ziidi Trader guide](/blog/ziidi-trader-complete-guide-kenya/) if you are comparing ways to access NSE shares. Always verify whether the particular share and transaction you want are supported by your chosen provider.

The CMA has warned investors about unlicensed operators. Verify a provider's status independently and never transfer investment money to an unknown personal account because someone promises guaranteed share returns. See the [CMA investor protection information](https://www.cma.or.ke/investor-protection/).

## What about IPOs and newly listed shares?

An initial public offering (IPO) lets investors apply for shares being offered to the public. A newly listed share can attract strong attention, but a successful listing or a familiar company name does not automatically make the offer price attractive.

For an IPO, examine the **prospectus, offer valuation, use of proceeds, existing shareholder sales, financial history, risks and how many shares may be available to trade**. If the company has not yet reported reliable profits, a simple P/E comparison may not work. Also distinguish an announced IPO, an open offer and shares that are already trading.

The same rule applies to a company that has just listed: **understand the business and price before placing an order**.

## Common mistakes when choosing shares to buy

- **Looking only at today's share price.** KSh5 per share can be expensive relative to profit; KSh500 can be cheap relative to profit.
- **Treating low P/E or P/B as proof of value.** Weak businesses can stay cheap for good reasons.
- **Confusing earnings with dividends.** A company may earn KSh10 per share but distribute only KSh2, or nothing.
- **Chasing a share after a big rally.** A past gain says little about the return available at today's price.
- **Ignoring liquidity.** A quoted market price does not guarantee you can sell your holding at that price.
- **Relying on outdated ratios or analyst targets.** Prices and earnings change, so every valuation has a date.
- **Borrowing to buy shares.** Losses do not cancel your loan repayment obligations.
- **Buying because someone famous owns the stock.** Their entry price, risk tolerance and time horizon may be very different from yours.

## Final verdict: Which shares should you buy?

The right question is not simply **"Which NSE share is going up?"** It is **"Which business can I understand, value sensibly and afford to hold through uncertainty?"**

Start with **P/E and P/B**, the two ratios highlighted in the speeches, then test the numbers against ROE, earnings quality, cash flow, dividends, debt, liquidity and risks. Add analyst fair-value estimates only after understanding their assumptions.

For live market research, begin with the [NSE Market Dashboard](/nse-market-dashboard/). Use the checklist above to assess any share before deciding whether its current price offers value.
